## Question 1: Deliver Results (behavioral)

**Question:** Tell me about a time you improved forecast accuracy under significant pressure.

- **Situation (S):** At **Cardinal Health**, I supported a **$180M expense base**, and leadership flagged that our quarterly forecast had a gap risk — accuracy was sitting at **91%**, well below the standard expected for board-level reporting.
- **Task (T):** I was responsible for rebuilding the **driver-based forecasting model** and improving the quarterly outlook process using **Workday Adaptive Planning** and **Excel**.
- **Action (A):** I performed a root-cause analysis of prior forecast misses and identified that distribution costs and **SG&A allocations** were using flat-rate assumptions rather than activity-based drivers. I rebuilt the model in **Workday Adaptive Planning**, linking each cost category to its true business driver — volume for distribution, headcount for compensation, contract terms for vendor spend. I introduced a **three-scenario structure** (base, upside, downside) so leadership could toggle assumptions without manual rework, and I ran **sensitivity analysis** on the highest-volatility inputs. I also built **variance attribution logic** directly into the model to automate monthly close commentary.
- **Result (R):** Forecast accuracy improved from **91% to 96%** within two quarters. The quarterly outlook process became faster and more repeatable, and the model was recognized by the VP of Finance as the team's standard planning tool — directly reducing the number of reforecast cycles needed each year.

## Question 4: Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit (behavioral)

**Question:** Tell me about a time you disagreed with a senior stakeholder. How did you handle it?

- **Situation (S):** At **Cardinal Health**, a senior business leader wanted us to model a **5% SG&A reduction** in the quarterly outlook with no supporting operational changes — this was going to feed directly into a board-level presentation.
- **Task (T):** As the analyst responsible for the model's integrity, I had to decide whether to flag my concern or simply run the numbers as instructed.
- **Action (A):** I pulled **8 quarters of SG&A trend data** using **SQL** and **Excel** and ran a detailed cost-category analysis. The data clearly showed there was no business driver — headcount, vendor contracts, or run-rate — that supported a 5% reduction. I prepared a two-page summary of my findings and proposed an alternative: a **2.5% reduction scenario** tied to specific efficiency levers that were actually in-flight. I presented both scenarios to my manager with documented rationale and asked for the decision to be escalated rather than embedded without discussion.
- **Result (R):** Leadership chose a conservative **2% reduction** in the final model, which proved much closer to actuals than the original assumption. The experience built credibility with my manager and reinforced that protecting the integrity of financial models is more valuable than avoiding conflict.

## Question 7: Dive Deep (behavioral)

**Question:** Tell me about a time you dug deeper than the surface data to uncover a key insight.

- **Situation (S):** At **Humana**, during a quarterly **medical cost review**, our commercial membership book was showing higher-than-expected costs. The initial summary attributed the variance to **inpatient spend** — but the magnitude didn't match what I was seeing in the raw claims trends.
- **Task (T):** I needed to break down the cost trend at a more granular level to identify the true driver and give the pricing and actuarial teams an accurate picture.
- **Action (A):** I went back to the raw claims data and used **SQL** to pull a detailed cost breakdown segmented by claim type, provider category, and member cohort. The drill-down revealed that the variance was not driven by inpatient volume at all — it was coming from a specific **outpatient procedure category** where a subset of high-cost providers had repriced. I cross-referenced against enrollment data to rule out member-mix shift as a factor, and built a clear **variance attribution waterfall in Excel** showing each driver's contribution to the total **PMPM** gap.
- **Result (R):** The analysis redirected the actuarial team's focus entirely. They adjusted their trend assumptions for the next rate cycle and avoided over-attributing the variance to inpatient utilization — a correction that would have had meaningful downstream impact on **premium rate planning**. This is a direct example of why I always go to the claim-level data rather than trusting summary roll-ups.

## Question 8: Bias for Action (hypothetical)

**Question:** The business needs a financial decision but the data is ambiguous and incomplete. What do you do?

- **Situation (S):** At **Cardinal Health**, supply chain disruptions in late 2024 created unusual payment timing shifts. Leadership needed a **cash flow and working capital forecast** within 48 hours to support a treasury decision — but there was no clean historical precedent to anchor the model.
- **Task (T):** I had to develop a credible, defensible forecast under time pressure without the luxury of clean, comparable data.
- **Action (A):** My first step was to inventory what data I did have: partial actuals, vendor contract terms, and procurement run-rates. I used these as anchors rather than waiting for complete data. I built a **scenario-based model in Excel** with three cases — baseline using current payment timing, a stress case assuming further delays, and a recovery case assuming normalization. Where data was missing, I used **regression modeling** to extrapolate from partial inputs and flagged all assumptions explicitly with confidence levels. I ran **sensitivity analysis** on key variables and aligned with treasury and procurement to pressure-test the directional logic before finalizing. I was clear in my output: here is what we know, here is what we assumed, and here is the range of outcomes.
- **Result (R):** The model was delivered within the deadline. Leadership appreciated the structured transparency — the separation of actuals from assumptions allowed them to make a real-time decision with appropriate risk awareness. The stress case proved closest to actual, which validated the approach and built confidence in scenario-based forecasting under uncertainty.

## Question 10: Are Right, A Lot (behavioral)

**Question:** Tell me about a time you found an error in a financial model. How did you find it and what did you do?

- **Situation (S):** At **Infinite Infolab**, I was reviewing the **revenue forecasting model** used for subscription and implementation streams during a quarterly reforecast cycle. The totals looked slightly off compared to what I expected based on contract data I had reviewed separately.
- **Task (T):** I was responsible for forecast accuracy, so I needed to trace the discrepancy to its source before the numbers were submitted.
- **Action (A):** I reconciled the model output line by line against the underlying data in **SQL**. I identified that a **VLOOKUP formula in Excel** was referencing a column range that had shifted after a table was reformatted — causing certain implementation revenue streams to be excluded from the total. The error had been in place for two prior quarters but was masked by actuals volatility. I rebuilt the lookup logic using a more robust **INDEX-MATCH formula** and added a **validation check** that automatically flags any instance where total model revenue differs from the sum of its inputs by more than 0.5%. I documented every change with audit-trail notes and revised the prior two quarters for context.
- **Result (R):** Correcting the error revealed revenue had been **understated by approximately 14%** in prior forecasts. I presented the revised history to my manager, and we updated the submission accordingly. I then proposed a **model review checklist** — including formula audits and cross-validation checks — that the team now uses before every forecast submission.

## Question 13: Financial Analysis (functional)

**Question:** Walk me through how you conduct variance analysis — from data pull to communication.

- **Situation (S):** I follow a structured approach that moves from summary to granular, always focused on identifying root causes and actionable insights rather than just describing the numbers.
- **Task (T):** At **Humana**, I do this monthly for **medical cost and MLR variance**. At **Cardinal Health**, I did it for expense and **working capital**.
- **Action (A):** My process starts at the **MLR level** — comparing actual medical loss ratio to budget and prior year, broken out by commercial membership segment. Once I identify which segments are driving the variance, I go one level deeper into **PMPM cost trends** by care category: inpatient, outpatient, pharmacy, and professional fees. I use **SQL** to pull detailed claims data segmented by claim type, provider category, and member cohort — this lets me split **price variance** (unit cost changes) from **volume variance** (utilization shifts), which is critical for understanding whether the issue is an actuarial assumption or an operational one. I build a **variance attribution waterfall in Excel or Power BI** and present a "so what" for each driver — for example, flagging whether a provider repricing event is a one-time issue or a recurring trend. At Cardinal Health, the same logic applied to **SG&A and segment P&L variance**, using **Workday Adaptive Planning** alongside Excel for cost-center-level drill-down.
- **Result (R):** The key principle across all settings is materiality — I don't spend equal time on every line item. I prioritize by dollar impact and recurrence, so the analysis surfaces the highest-impact findings clearly and the output is always actionable, not just descriptive.

## Question 14: Data Management (functional)

**Question:** How do you handle ambiguous data, multiple data sources, and data cleaning in practice?

- **Situation (S):** At **Humana**, I regularly pull and combine three primary data sets — **claims data, enrollment records, and provider cost data** — which live in different systems at different granularities. This is a real-world messy data problem every month.
- **Task (T):** My job is to produce clean, analytically sound inputs for **medical cost forecasting** and **MLR analysis**, which means the data quality work is inseparable from the analytical work.
- **Action (A):** My workflow starts with understanding each source's grain. Claims are at the claim-line level, enrollment is at the member-month level, and provider data is at the contract or encounter level. I use **SQL** to perform all joins, standardizing to a **member-month grain** before any aggregation. Before merging, I run quality checks — flagging duplicates, unexpected nulls, and outlier values that may indicate system issues. When data is ambiguous — for example, a claim spanning multiple service dates or an unclear provider attribution — I document the assumption explicitly and flag it for review rather than silently imputing or dropping records. For mid-size datasets, I use **MS Access** for joins that are too large for **Excel** but don't require a full SQL server. For transformations and cleaning in Excel, I use **Power Query**. I maintain a clear **data lineage log** so that every metric in the final output can be traced to a source record.
- **Result (R):** At **Cardinal Health**, I applied the same discipline when cleaning distribution cost data across multiple **ERP feeds** — identifying and resolving timing differences between accruals and actual payments. The principle is the same in every context: never trust the data until you've tested it, and always leave an audit trail.

## Question 15: Gen AI Fluency (functional)

**Question:** How have you used Gen AI in your finance work, and where do you see its biggest potential?

- **Situation (S):** I'm not an AI expert, but I've been deliberate about identifying where **Gen AI** genuinely adds value in **FP&A workflows** versus where it creates noise — and I've been actively testing both.
- **Task (T):** My goal has been to use AI as a productivity accelerator for experienced analysts, not a replacement for financial judgment.
- **Action (A):** At **Humana**, I've used AI-assisted tools to generate first-draft commentary for **monthly close packages**. After pulling variance data in **SQL** and building my attribution waterfall, I use Gen AI to produce a structured first draft of the narrative — which I then validate, edit, and finalize. This cuts documentation time meaningfully while keeping the analyst in control of the output. I've also used AI to assist with **SQL query drafting** — for complex queries involving multiple joins or window functions, AI generates an initial version that I then test against our actual data and optimize. More broadly, I see significant near-term potential in three areas: **anomaly detection** in large claims or commodity cost datasets, **scenario narrative generation** (converting model output into plain-language summaries for different audiences), and **forecast assumption documentation** (automatically generating explainers for assumption changes between forecast cycles). In supply chain finance specifically — relevant to **AWS infrastructure** — AI could flag procurement patterns deviating from historical norms, helping analysts focus attention on the highest-signal items in a large dataset.
- **Result (R):** The lens I apply is always **human-in-the-loop**: AI enhances analytical judgment, it doesn't replace it. The analyst must understand the data, validate the logic, and remain accountable for the output. That principle has guided how I evaluate and adopt any new tool.
